    Exploring a building nobody has mapped

    The swarm starts at the door with an empty map. Each drone senses, a central planner decides who goes where, and each drone flies its own path. That cycle repeats every second until there is nothing left to see.

    Sensing: a shared occupancy grid

    The building is a 200 × 140 grid of 20 cm cells. Each drone sweeps 180 lidar rays out to 8 m, ten times a second. Each ray walks the grid cell by cell (a DDA ray cast): cells it passes through become more likely free, and the cell it hits becomes more likely wall. Evidence is stored as log-odds, so repeated sightings add up and noise washes out. All drones write into the same map.

    Deciding where to look next: frontiers

    A frontier is open space that touches the unknown. The planner groups frontier cells into clusters and scores each cluster for each drone: the shortest flight distance to reach it, minus a bonus for how much unknown it borders. Those scores form a cost matrix, and the Hungarian algorithm finds the assignment of drones to frontiers with the lowest total cost, so two drones don't chase the same gap. A drone keeps its target unless another is clearly better.

    Getting there: paths through what is known

    Walls are inflated by the drone's safety margin. Flight distances come from Dijkstra over the grid, with unseen cells allowed at 2.5 times the cost, so drones prefer known space but can plan into the unknown. Paths are shortened by line-of-sight checks. Drones push each other apart when they get within a metre.

    Coming home

    Each drone checks whether it has the battery to get back. If not, it returns, recharges and rejoins. Take a drone down mid-flight and the planner reassigns its frontier at the next cycle. When no frontiers remain, or none can be reached, the swarm lands at base.

    What is simplified

    Drones know their own position (as if from ultra-wideband beacons), the map is shared instantly, and the building is flat.
